Reflective Piece – Security & Risk Management Module

Using Rolfe’s (2001) model: What / So What / Now What

e-Portfolio URL: https://mohammed1khalfan.github.io/e-Portfolio/

What

Throughout this module, I have developed a more systematic and analytical understanding of security and risk management, especially how modern frameworks support decision-making in increasingly complex digital ecosystems. In the early units, I began by examining fundamental concepts such as Industry 4.0, automation, and digital transformation. I learned that emerging technologies like IoT, AI, and machine learning create new efficiencies but also expand the attack surface of organisations (Spring et al., 2021). My engagement in discussions on industrial security incidents, such as the NotPetya attack on Maersk, gave me an appreciation for how cyber threats can rapidly cascade through global supply chains (Greenberg, 2018).

By Unit 6, the learning transitioned from theory to structured analysis through the collaborative project Risk Identification Report for Pampered Pets. I participated actively in identifying and classifying operational, financial, and technological risks using the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. This phase introduced me to qualitative risk matrices, prioritisation techniques, and mitigation strategies. It was my first experience working in a small distributed team where each member contributed distinct expertise. Completing the peer evaluation improved my awareness of teamwork accountability and how collaborative risk assessments can yield more balanced outcomes.

In Units 7–10, my focus shifted to vulnerability assessment frameworks. I explored the limitations of CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) and how contextual frameworks like SSVC (Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorisation) and EPSS (Exploit Prediction Scoring System) provide more adaptable and data-driven evaluation. These studies were particularly relevant to my professional role in data centre operations, where prioritising security patches or responding to vendor alerts often demands contextual judgement rather than mechanical scoring.

Finally, in Unit 11, I synthesised all learning into the final project Risk Assessment and Disaster Recovery Strategy for Cathy’s Company. This project required building a quantitative risk model using Monte Carlo simulation and FMEA to estimate probabilities of disruption and quality degradation across a new digital supply chain. I learned how to translate technical risks into measurable financial and operational impacts, enabling management-level decision-making. The project also demanded designing a Disaster Recovery (DR) and Business Continuity Plan (BCP) with recovery objectives, redundant suppliers, and regulatory compliance (GDPR).

So What

Reflecting on this experience, I can identify a significant evolution in how I perceive risk — moving from a checklist-based perspective to an integrated, system-thinking approach. Initially, my understanding of risk was largely reactive: identifying potential failures and assigning subjective severity levels. However, through the iterative discussions and projects, I began to realise that effective risk management must be context-aware, data-driven, and strategic (Spring et al., 2021).

Learning about SSVC was particularly transformative. I understood that unlike CVSS, which offers a static score, SSVC encourages dynamic decision-making based on exploitability, exposure, and mission criticality. This concept resonated strongly with my daily work in data centre operations, where not all vulnerabilities require the same urgency. I now see the importance of integrating both technical data (like EPSS predictions) and human judgement to determine mitigation priorities.

The collaborative work in Unit 6 also reshaped my understanding of teamwork in risk management. Participating in group activities required negotiating risk perspectives and aligning communication. This taught me that risk communication is as vital as risk calculation. Misunderstanding between stakeholders can itself become a vulnerability. The peer feedback process also revealed how psychological safety and transparent contribution tracking encourage productive teamwork.

The shift from qualitative to quantitative risk assessment during Unit 11 marked a pivotal turning point. Applying Monte Carlo simulation forced me to quantify uncertainty rather than describe it vaguely. I learned how small changes in variable assumptions can drastically alter overall risk exposure, reinforcing the necessity of sensitivity analysis. The Disaster Recovery and BCP planning aspect also demonstrated that resilience is not only about preventing incidents but ensuring continuity and recovery, especially when tied to measurable RTO and RPO metrics.

Now What

Looking ahead, I intend to integrate quantitative risk assessment and SSVC-based prioritisation into my data centre operations. Instead of treating every alert with equal urgency, I will apply a tiered response strategy using exploit prediction data to prioritise incidents. I also plan to adopt continuous risk dashboards that visualise both cyber and operational indicators — bridging analytics with incident management.

Additionally, I will enhance collaboration across teams by promoting structured risk communication frameworks. Applying similar documentation and peer review processes in my workplace can strengthen coordination between L1 Support, Network Operations, and Projects Teams during risk reviews or CAB (Change Advisory Board) meetings. Academically, this module has encouraged me to explore advanced risk analytics, including Bayesian inference and machine-learning-based risk forecasting.

From a personal standpoint, I have gained confidence in writing academically, using Harvard referencing and reflective models such as Rolfe (2001). I also recognise that ethical and professional integrity remain central to both academia and information security. The emphasis on academic honesty reminded me that integrity extends beyond systems — it defines professional credibility.

In summary, this module transformed my technical perspective into a holistic, evidence-based understanding of risk and resilience. It connected theory with practice, enabling me to evolve from analysing vulnerabilities to designing systems of prevention, response, and recovery. I aim to continue applying these principles — merging critical thinking, technical precision, and ethical responsibility — to manage the evolving landscape of digital risk.
