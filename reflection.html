Reflective Piece – Security & Risk Management


What
I have taken away a much more structured and analytic perspective of security and risk management the way that contemporary models inform decision making in sophisticated digital environments. In the early modules, I looked at basic ideas such as Industry 4.0, automation and digital transformation.” I was reading about how new IoT, AI and machine learning technologies provide greater efficiency yet increase the attack surface for companies (Spring et al., 2021). My participation facilitating industrial security incident discussions, including the NotPetya attack on Maersk, introduced me to the concept of a cyber threat causing knock-on effects that cascade through global supply chains in real time (Greenberg, 2018).
In Unit 6 it moved from theory to more practical work with the collaborative project Risk Identification Report for Pampered Pets. I played an active role in the discovery and categorization of critical operational, financial & technology risks using the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. This phase taught me some about qualitative risk matrices and prioritisation techniques / mitigation approaches. First time working at small distributed team where everyone has a unique expertise of his own. The peer review process has further enhanced my understanding of the need for accountability in teamwork, and that group risk assessments enable more robust findings.
While being in Units 7–10, I turned my attention towards the state of play of vulnerability assessment frameworks. I considered shortcomings of CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) and showed how context driven frameworks like SSVC (Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorisation) and EPSS (Exploit Prediction Scoring System) lead to more flexible, evidence based assessment. Such studies were particularly applicable to my day job working in data center operations, where scoring isn't always purely mechanical and where the decision of how soon to apply security patches or act on vendor notifications can involve a good deal of context.
Finally in Unit 11 I put all my learning together into the final project Risk Assessment and Disater Recovery Strategy for Cathy’s Company. This project involved designing a quantitative risk model utilising Monte Carlo simulation and combining FMEA estimates on the probabilities of different disruptions and quality problems in a new digital supply chain. I figured out how you could quantize technical risks in terms of financial and operational consequences, so that management could actually make decisions. They also required to design a DR/BCP plan with RTOs, double suppliers and GDPR compliance.

So What
When looking back at this experience I can clearly see a huge change in my perception of Risk from checklist based to systemic thinking. At first, my concept of risk was mostly reactional: finding weak points and determining how bad to feel about them. But as we made our way through the iterative conversations and projects, I came to understand that effective risk management is both context-sensitive, evidence-based, and strategic (Spring et al., 2021).
It’s been an especially transformative experience being introduced to SSVC. I realized that while CVSS has more of a static score, with an SSVC it tries to move us into more dynamic determination based on exploitability, exposure, and mission criticality. This really struck a chord with me, having to deal with data centre operations on a daily basis – not all vulnerabilities are created equal in terms of urgency. I now realize the need of including simply technical data (such as EPSS prediction) and human judgment in the prioritization process to mitigate systems.
The teamwork in Unit 6 humbled my view of team work around risk-mgmt also. Engagement in group activities involved risk negotiation and communication alignment. This helped me understand that risk communication is as important as risk calculation. Miscommunication can in itself be a weakness. The peer feedback process also illustrated how feelings of psychological safety and open tracking of contribution promote effective teamwork.
The transition from qualitative to quantitative risk assessment at Unit 11 was a turning point. (The use of Monte Carlo simulation made me more quantitative and less vague on describing uncertainty.) I saw how a tiny change in the assumptions about a variable dramatically changes overall risk, again reinforcing that you must be sensitive to uncertainty. The DRBCP planning too proved that resilience is not all about avoiding incidents but carrying on and recovering from them, especially when we attached quantifiable RTO and RPO metrics.

Now What
In the future, I am planning to incorporate quantitative risk assessment and SSVC based prioritisation into my data center operations. I’m not going to respond as if everything is critical, I’m going to have a response tier set using exploit prediction data instead. I’ll also be doing continuous risk dashboards covering cyber metrics and operational ones too linking analytics to incident management.
I will also help foster Engagement not just with us as a team, but our risk partners and enhance this across teams by promoting structured Risk communication frameworks. Utilizing the same sort of documentation /peer review processes in my work place would help making sure that L1 Support, Network Operations and Projects Teams are well coordinated as an early warning system on risk reviews or CAB meetings. I have also become interested (from an academic standpoint) to delve into more sophisticated risk analytics such as Bayesian inference & machine learning based predictive modeling of risk.
Personally, I have also grown to feel confident writing academically, with Harvard referencing and reflection through Rolfe et al (2001). Finally, I accept the importance of ethical and professional standards throughout academia and information security. The focus on academic integrity reminded me that integrity surpasses systems it is a disciplinary credibility.
In closing, this was module that altered my stubborn left brain into a more balanced perspective of risk and resilience. It was the link between theory and practice, allowing me to move from study of vulnerabilities to design of prevention, response and recovery systems. I plan to keep practising these principles of critical thinking, technical accuracy and ethical integrity in order to address the shifting sands of digital risk.
